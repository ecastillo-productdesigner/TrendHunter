Men Are Challenging the Midlife 'Just Aging' Narrative as Testosterone Therapy Gains Momentum

By Samantha Agate

Testosterone replacement therapy has moved out of the anti-aging clinic and into the open, with doctors and patients publicly documenting the treatment as a fix for the fatigue, weight gain and low libido that primary care physicians have long chalked up to normal midlife decline.

The stories share a common arc — dismissal, second opinions, blood work — and they are reshaping how men in their 40s and 50s think about hormone health. What began as a fringe conversation is now a consumer category, and the next 18 months will decide how far it goes.

What’s Happening

Personal TRT accounts are proliferating online, from clinician blogs to major men’s magazines. Men’s Health recently profiled 22 men on testosterone therapy, documenting cases across ages, health histories and dosing routines. Dr. Jeffrey Arbuckle, a physician with more than 36 years of clinical experience, published his own account on the Fountain of You MD site, describing a testosterone level of 260 ng/dL at age 50 alongside high blood pressure, elevated cholesterol, borderline diabetes and sleep apnea.

Since starting weekly injections, Arbuckle says he has lost 75 pounds and resolved his hypertension, sleep apnea, high cholesterol and elevated blood sugar. Jason Holmes, a 46-year-old profiled by Men’s Health, injects testosterone twice weekly to keep his levels between roughly 750 and 900 ng/dL and has lost 120 pounds over two years. Charles Flanagan, who resumed therapy after his level dropped below 100 ng/dL, told the magazine, “At 46, I’m in the best shape I’ve ever been.”

Why It’s Happening

Three forces are pushing TRT into the mainstream.

The first is a cultural rejection of the “just aging” script. Dr. Stefaan Vossen, founder of the Core clinic in Warwickshire, told The Guardian that even with medical training he “bought into the cultural narrative that with a busy lifestyle, these symptoms are nothing more than normal ageing.” A GP told him the same. Only after seven months of diet, sleep and exercise fixes did a second opinion find his testosterone was roughly half of what it should have been.

The second is a widening gap between the clinical “normal range” and how men actually feel. Arbuckle’s 260 ng/dL was technically inside the low end of normal. Tim, a 47-year-old documented on Numan, was told by the NHS his levels were normal and was prescribed antidepressants; private testing on a different scale flagged him as low. He now urges friends to retest even if a first result reads normal, because “testosterone levels fluctuate so much.”

The third is telehealth infrastructure. Jacob Samuel, 82, monitors his levels through quarterly blood work on the platform Opt Health and applies a daily testosterone cream. Holmes turned to an online TRT clinic after his primary care doctor waved off results in the 300s as age-related.

What Could Be Next

Expect TRT to keep moving away from urology offices and toward direct-to-consumer care, with three shifts likely to define the next stage.

Primary care will lose ground to online clinics for hormone care. The pattern is repeating across the source cases: men present symptoms, get told they’re aging or depressed and eventually route around their GP to a specialty telehealth provider. That workflow is now familiar enough — and successful enough — that it will accelerate. Reader action: If you’re experiencing fatigue, weight gain or libido changes, ask for a full hormone panel by name (total and free testosterone, SHBG, estradiol) rather than a general wellness draw, and get a second reading. A single number near the low end of normal is not a full picture, as Tim’s case makes clear.

Combination protocols — TRT plus GLP-1 plus lifestyle — will become the default, not the outlier. Holmes injects testosterone twice weekly, but his 120-pound loss came only after he restructured nutrition around 225 grams of protein a day, trained six days a week, cut alcohol by 90 percent and added a low-dose GLP-1. Arbuckle puts it bluntly: “You can’t out-inject poor habits.” As GLP-1 access expands, expect stacked prescribing to become standard in private hormone clinics like the ones profiled by The Guardian. Reader action: Before starting TRT, decide whether you’re prepared to overhaul training, protein intake and alcohol use. If not, budget for slower results and don’t blame the hormone.

The definition of “normal” testosterone will come under real pressure. Three of the source cases — Arbuckle at 260 ng/dL, Holmes in the 300s and Tim inside the NHS range — involved men who were symptomatic at numbers their doctors called fine. That mismatch is now visible enough, and often enough replicated online, that patient demand will push clinicians and labs to revisit thresholds. Reader action: Get a baseline reading in your 30s or early 40s while you feel well, so you have your own comparison point if symptoms show up later. Don’t rely on a lab’s population range to tell you what’s normal for you.

The takeaway is straightforward: TRT is no longer a fringe intervention, and the men driving its growth are the ones who refused to accept a shrug from their doctor. If you’re in that category, do the testing, do the reading and expect any real transformation to require work beyond the needle.

THREE SIGNALS TO WATCH:

  1. New or revised clinical guidelines from the Endocrine Society or American Urological Association on testosterone thresholds and screening.
  2. A major insurer publicly expanding or restricting coverage for testosterone testing and TRT prescribing through telehealth platforms.
  3. FDA action on combination prescribing that pairs testosterone with GLP-1 medications through direct-to-consumer clinics.
