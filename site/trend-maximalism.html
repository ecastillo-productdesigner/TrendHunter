Maximalism Is the Top Home Decor Trend of 2026: What's Happening, Why and What It Means Going Forward

By Ryan Brennan

August 17, 2026 1:03 PM

If your Pinterest boards look nothing like the matched furniture set your parents keep suggesting, the data is on your side. Maximalism is displacing the showroom-neutral home, and the version taking hold is more collected than crammed. Search trends, designer surveys and celebrity interiors all point to a look built on personal history. The next phase isn’t about filling rooms. It’s about editing them.

Maximalism Is Becoming More Personal, Collected and Lived-In

Searches for “vintage maximalism” rose 260%, “eclectic maximalism” rose 215% and “eclectic apartment” rose 630%, according to Pinterest’s 2025 Fall Trends report. In the 1stDibs Interior Designer Trends Survey, maximalism and eclecticism ranked as the two most in-demand styles of 2026, cited by 39% and 38% of the 468 design professionals polled.

The look even has a nickname, the “eccentric aunt aesthetic.” Kelsie Lally, owner and lead designer of Ameena Design, told MarthaStewart.com that the style “embraces individuality, layered collections, rich color, books, antiques, textiles, original artwork and unexpected combinations that reveal the personality of the person who lives there.”

Translation for anyone who just closed on a starter home. The shift moves away from filling every surface and toward editing meaningful pieces together, like an inherited cabinet next to a contemporary lamp, or a flea-market chair beside family photographs. One shelf or one gallery wall is usually the starting point, not a whole-room overhaul.

Why Maximalism Home Design Is Resonating Right Now

Three forces are driving the shift. The first is fatigue with sameness. Stephanie Hunt, founder and creative director of The Flairhunter, told Forbes, “Maximalism is having another moment, with an emphasis on special pieces and pops of really, truly personal items.”

Permission to mix is the second. Autumn Pochiro, founder of Autumn Dawn Design, told MarthaStewart.com, “No color or pattern is off-limits. If you love it, include it.” She added that placement and the juxtaposition of color and texture are what let a room tell a story.

Visibility is the third. Johnny Galecki’s home outside Nashville, where velvet seating, oversized portraits, ceiling wallpaper, an antique Persian rug and a chrome-and-wood pool table share one family room, has given the aesthetic a high-profile reference point. Nicole Byer has done something similar with her tiger-print couch, telling The Ethos, “Make the place yours. And also, who cares what other people like? You have to like it. You have to look at it. You have to live in it.”

What Could Be Next for Maximalism Design in Homes

Expect maximalism’s next phase to be defined less by what people buy and more by what they keep, edit and rotate.

Slow decorating overtakes the shopping spree. The eccentric aunt look already leans on inherited furniture, flea-market finds and objects accumulated over years. That points toward rooms built gradually, not assembled in a single weekend haul from one furniture store. Galecki’s yellow chair keeps its scratches and rips. His vintage pool table looks played on. Visible wear is starting to read as authenticity rather than disrepair.

Try this before buying new. Audit what you already own, because a scratched hand-me-down or an estate-sale lamp may be the anchor a room needs. Set a rule that at least half of any redecorated space comes from secondhand, inherited or already-owned pieces.

Celebrity interiors normalize weirder taste. Galecki’s house stands out because his designers refused to smooth him out. Emily Ward told Architectural Digest, “Johnny is just one of those people that forces you more outside of the box than you already thought you were.” Louisa Pierce added, “I’d say he’s one of the only clients that we’ve ever had that has been like, I want this to be more interesting.” If more aspirational tours follow that lead, the polished neutral celebrity home may become the outlier.

Rework your inspiration feed. When collecting references, save homes built around collections and unusual color rather than the glossy showroom tours. Your camera roll shapes what feels normal.

Editing becomes the actual skill. As more people adopt the style, keeping layered rooms intentional rather than chaotic will separate the successful attempts from the cluttered ones. Morgan Cartier Weston, founder of Cocoon Design Studio, told Good Housekeeping, “If you’re not ready to fully jump into this aesthetic, dip your toe in by mixing up your picture frames, starting a collection of something that brings you joy or covering your sofa in mismatched throw pillows.” Designer Whitney Anderson told the same outlet, “Rather than a one-size-fits-all approach, this is one-size-fits-YOU.”

Give every collection a defined home, whether one shelf, one wall or one console, and preserve some empty tabletop and wall space. Rotate displays seasonally so the room reads as edited, not accumulated.

The bottom line. The maximalist homes that win the next two years won’t be the fullest ones. They’ll be the hardest to copy, because the person living there is what gives the room its point of view.

  1. Pinterest’s next trends report, and whether “vintage maximalism” and “eclectic apartment” search growth accelerates or flattens.
  2. The 2027 edition of the 1stDibs Interior Designer Trends Survey, and whether maximalism holds its top-style ranking or begins to slip.
  3. New celebrity home tours in Architectural Digest, tracking how many lean into collected, vintage-heavy interiors versus reverting to neutral palettes.
